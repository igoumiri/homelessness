\begin{abstract}
This report presents a comprehensive, data-driven assessment of Continuum of Care (CoC) effectiveness in combating homelessness among older adults (65+) across California. The analysis leverages publicly available data, rigorous statistical modeling, and an innovative interpretation methodology to identify factors influencing homelessness and to provide actionable insights for policy and resource allocation. By integrating diverse datasets including homelessness counts, population demographics, and geographical information, and employing both Random Forest and ANCOVA models, we aim to uncover the underlying drivers of homelessness in this vulnerable demographic. A key innovation is the "prediction set" approach, which enables a counterfactual comparison of CoC performance by standardizing confounding variables. Our findings highlight the critical role of housing affordability and economic stability, provide a ranking of CoCs based on their effectiveness under controlled conditions, and offer concrete policy recommendations for targeted interventions and best practice dissemination. This study contributes to a more transparent and evidence-based approach to addressing California's escalating homelessness crisis.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

This report presents a comprehensive, data-driven assessment of Continuum of Care (CoC) effectiveness in combating homelessness among older adults (65+) across California. The analysis leverages publicly available data, rigorous statistical modeling, and an innovative interpretation methodology to identify factors influencing homelessness and to provide actionable insights for policy and resource allocation.

\subsection{The escalating crisis of homelessness in California}

California faces a profound and escalating crisis of homelessness, bearing a disproportionate burden compared to the rest of the United States. In 2024, the Public Policy Institute of California reported over 187,000 people experiencing homelessness in California, comprising approximately 12\% of the nation's population but representing 24\% of the national total, with two out of three of these individuals being unsheltered~\cite{ppic2025homelessness}. This scale of homelessness highlights a pervasive societal challenge.

Recent trends underscore the worsening nature of this crisis. Between 2020 and 2022, homelessness in California increased by 6\%, translating to nearly 10,000 more individuals experiencing homelessness~\cite{calich2025hdis}. This persistent growth is primarily attributed to systemic issues rather than isolated individual circumstances. The severe housing affordability crisis, characterized by rising home prices and rents outpacing new construction, stands out as a fundamental driver~\cite{ppic2025homelessness}. A significant portion of the population, over a quarter of all Californians and four in ten lower-income adults, express daily or near-daily concern about their housing costs~\cite{ppic2025homelessness}. This economic pressure demonstrates that while individual vulnerabilities, such as mental health issues or substance use, are often associated with homelessness~\cite{terner2023older}, the sheer magnitude and expansion of the problem are deeply rooted in structural failures of the housing market~\cite{huduser2019market}. This perspective shifts the understanding of homelessness from primarily one of individual pathology to one of systemic inequity, emphasizing that effective policy interventions must fundamentally address these underlying economic and housing supply issues.

A critical challenge in addressing this crisis is the lack of comprehensive evaluation of existing programs. Despite the investment of ``tens of billions of dollars" by California lawmakers into homelessness programs in recent years, a statewide audit found insufficient information to assess their effectiveness~\cite{ppic2025homelessness}. This reveals a significant gap in accountability and a pressing need for robust, data-driven frameworks to evaluate interventions. The present study, by aiming to identify effective CoCs, directly contributes to addressing this need for transparency and evidence-based policy.

\subsection{Unique challenges and growing vulnerability of older adults (65+) experiencing homelessness}

Within the broader homelessness crisis, older adults, particularly those aged 65 and over, represent a rapidly growing and uniquely vulnerable demographic. The proportion of people experiencing homelessness aged 65 and older in the United States is projected to more than double by 2030 \cite{governing2024aging}. Adults aged 50 and above now constitute nearly 40\% of those seeking shelter in California \cite{terner2023older, ucsfhomelessness2024older}. From 2017 to 2022, the number of adults 65 and older accessing homelessness services surged by over 166\%, a rate exceeding any other age group \cite{terner2023older}. In 2023, approximately 38,028 individuals aged 55 and older experienced homelessness in the state \cite{terner2023older}.

A notable characteristic of this demographic is the prevalence of late-onset homelessness, with a significant proportion (41\%) of older adults experiencing homelessness for the first time after the age of 50 \cite{terner2023older, cmaj2024late}. This often stems from economic hardship, housing insecurity, and financial, familial, or medical emergencies occurring later in life, rather than solely from chronic issues \cite{terner2023older, governing2024aging, urbaninstitute2025housing}. Financial factors, including unaffordable housing costs, fixed incomes, stagnant wages, high healthcare expenditures, and the loss of pensions, are increasingly identified as primary drivers of elder homelessness, often outweighing mental health problems or substance abuse \cite{capitolweekly2024crisis, justiceinaging2024unaffordability, usich2025prevention}. Indeed, over half of older adult renters in California struggle with unaffordable housing costs \cite{terner2023older, justiceinaging2024unaffordability}.

The experiences of older adults experiencing homelessness are further complicated by compounded vulnerabilities. A substantial majority, 72\%, report having a disabling condition, which often necessitates tailored interventions and specialized support \cite{terner2023older, ucsfnews2016geriatric, p4bhealth2024aging}. Research indicates that people experiencing homelessness develop manifestations of advanced age and typically face geriatric conditions decades earlier than housed adults of the same chronological age \cite{ucsfnews2016geriatric, pmc2018symptoms}. Moreover, stark racial disparities persist within this population. Black Californians, who comprise roughly 5.4\% of the state's total population, were disproportionately represented, making up 26\% of older adults (50+) who accessed homeless services in 2022-23 \cite{terner2023older, calbudgetcenter2025rise}. Similarly, Indigenous individuals (0.3\% of the population, 2.0\% of unhoused 50+) and Pacific Islanders (0.4\% of the population, 0.8\% of unhoused 50\%) are also significantly overrepresented \cite{terner2023older}. This evidence indicates that older adults experiencing homelessness are not a homogenous group; rather, the convergence of age, race, disability, and economic precarity creates a complex web of vulnerabilities. The severe overrepresentation of Black, Indigenous, and Pacific Islander older adults points to deep-seated systemic inequities, such as historical discrimination in housing and employment, which exacerbate the challenges of aging. This implies that effective interventions for older adults must adopt an intersectional lens, being culturally competent and specifically addressing these compounded disadvantages.

The finding that a substantial portion of older adults experience homelessness for the first time later in life, coupled with financial factors being primary drivers, strongly suggests that a shift towards proactive prevention strategies is crucial for this demographic \cite{terner2023older, cmaj2024late, usich2025prevention}. This aligns with successful models such as the California Policy Lab's pioneering work in using predictive analytics for homelessness prevention in Los Angeles, which proactively identifies and supports those at highest risk \cite{capolicylab2025hpu}. By identifying CoCs that demonstrate greater or lesser effectiveness, this study can inform where such proactive, targeted measures—including financial assistance and housing retention support—might yield the greatest impact for older adults.

\subsection{The role of Continuums of Care (CoCs) in homelessness response}

Continuums of Care (CoCs) serve as the cornerstone of the coordinated response to homelessness in the United States. These are federally mandated and funded local or regional planning bodies, established by the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD), responsible for coordinating homelessness services and administering community-wide programs aimed at ending homelessness and increasing self-sufficiency~\cite{huduser2019market, huduser2000coc}. The CoC approach, implemented by HUD in 1995, was specifically designed to streamline funding processes and foster more integrated local responses to homelessness~\cite{huduser2000coc}.

Evaluations of the CoC model indicate that high-performing CoCs have demonstrated increased communication and better-coordinated programs, leading to a greater number of individuals receiving services and participating in integrated support systems~\cite{huduser2000coc}. While CoCs are designed to provide a coordinated response, their effectiveness can vary significantly. This heterogeneity in performance is a critical area for investigation. Research suggests that CoC performance can be influenced by both internal network dynamics, such as board composition, leadership structures, and the level of HUD funding received, as well as external system contexts, including the prevailing political climate, median rent levels, and the existing homeless population ratio~\cite{preprints2025coc}. The objective of this study, to compare CoC effectiveness, directly addresses this observed variability, aiming to uncover the underlying factors that contribute to differential outcomes across California's CoCs.

\subsection{Research objectives and distinctive aspects of our data-driven, statistically rigorous approach}

This study aims to leverage publicly available data on homelessness, population demographics, and geographical characteristics to train and rigorously analyze statistical models. The primary goal is to predict and evaluate the effectiveness of California's Continuums of Care (CoCs) in combating homelessness, with a specific focus on the highly vulnerable 65+ age group. The research seeks to identify which CoCs are demonstrating greater effectiveness, given their contextual factors, and to provide interpretable insights for policy and resource allocation.

While building on established methodologies, the approach employed in this study offers several distinctive contributions to the field of homelessness research. Early work in this domain was often hindered by ``definitional inconsistencies and data limitations," frequently leading to ``largely speculative" conclusions \cite{researchgate2009history}. However, significant ``technical improvements and explicit attention to how aspects of research design shape results" have emerged since the mid-1990s, paving the way for more robust analyses \cite{researchgate2009history}.

Existing data-driven approaches to understanding and addressing homelessness include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item HUD studies that have utilized empirical models to identify market factors, such as rental costs and evictions, as significant predictors of homelessness rates across CoCs nationally, noting distinct regional differences \cite{huduser2019market}.
    \item The U.S. Government Accountability Office (GAO) has employed econometric analysis of Point-in-Time (PIT) count data, establishing a link between rental prices and homelessness, while also highlighting challenges in data quality and methodology \cite{culhane2024learning}.
    \item The California Policy Lab at UCLA has pioneered the use of predictive models with county-level data to identify individuals at high risk of homelessness, enabling proactive prevention efforts in Los Angeles County \cite{capolicylab2025hpu}.
    \item The UCSF Benioff Homelessness and Housing Initiative conducts large-scale, representative studies using mixed methods (surveys and in-depth interviews) to gain a deeper understanding of the experiences of people experiencing homelessness in California \cite{ucsf2025studies}.
    \item Recent academic work has applied regression analysis to a national sample of 343 CoC networks to examine factors influencing their performance, particularly for Permanent Supportive Housing services, emphasizing the importance of robust statistical methods and larger sample sizes for generalizability \cite{preprints2025coc}.
    \item Comparative analyses of homelessness interventions in major cities often incorporate data-driven approaches to identify effective strategies \cite{pubmed2021effectiveness}.
\end{itemize}

While data-driven analyses of homelessness are not new, the present study distinguishes itself through several key aspects:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The specific focus on the \textbf{65+ age group} within California's CoCs, a demographic experiencing rapid growth in homelessness and unique vulnerabilities.
    \item The \textbf{integration of diverse public datasets}—CoC-level homelessness data, multi-granular census population data, and geographical information—to create a rich, comprehensive dataset for analysis at a sub-state level.
    \item The \textbf{comparative application of both Random Forest and ANCOVA} to leverage their respective analytical strengths: Random Forest for capturing complex, non-linear relationships and providing overall feature importance, and ANCOVA for its ability to model interpretable linear effects and controlled comparisons.
    \item The innovative use of a \textbf{``prediction set" methodology} to standardize confounding variables (such as population) and enable a more equitable, counterfactual comparison of CoC effectiveness. This method moves beyond simple prediction to generate actionable, policy-relevant insights by ranking CoCs based on their performance under controlled conditions.
\end{itemize}

The core of this approach lies not merely in the application of statistical models, but in the explicit emphasis on interpreting their outputs for policy implications, particularly through the ``prediction set" approach. Machine learning interpretability is crucial for social policy evaluation \cite{molnar2022interpretable}, as it fosters trust, helps identify and debug biases, and increases the social acceptance of AI-driven decisions. By providing clear, actionable insights on CoC effectiveness, this study moves beyond simply predicting outcomes to delivering concrete guidance for policymakers.

\subsection{Homelessness Demographics Data: Source, Scope, and Limitations}

The primary source for homelessness demographics in California is the California Open Data portal, specifically the ``Homelessness Count By Age" dataset~\cite{caopendatahomelessness}. This data originates from the Homelessness Data Integration System (HDIS), a statewide data warehouse that collects and processes information from all 44 California Continuums of Care (CoCs)~\cite{calich2025hdis}. These CoCs are regional bodies responsible for coordinating homelessness services and planning interventions.

The dataset provides yearly statewide and CoC-level total counts of individuals receiving homeless response services. It offers breakdowns by key demographics, including age (with a crucial focus on the 65+ age group), race, gender, veteran status, and disability status~\cite{caopendatahomelessness}. The temporal coverage of this data spans from January 1, 2017, to September 30, 2024~\cite{caopendatahomelessness}. The definition of ``experiencing homelessness" used in this dataset is comprehensive, encompassing individuals who accessed lodging services (such as Emergency Shelter, Transitional Housing, and Safe Haven projects), those who entered permanent housing projects directly from homelessness, or individuals who reported living in a homeless situation (e.g., in a vehicle) while accessing other services~\cite{caopendatahomelessness}. This definition is an expanded version compared to the one used by the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) for its Annual Point-in-Time (PIT) Count, which typically focuses on those accessing shelter services~\cite{hudexchange2024pit}.

Despite its breadth, the homelessness data has inherent limitations that warrant careful consideration. HDIS primarily includes data from service providers participating in local Homeless Management Information Systems (HMIS) within CoCs. However, not all providers participate; for instance, independent providers without federal or certain state funding can opt out, and dedicated domestic violence victim service providers are legally prohibited from entering client data into HMIS, instead utilizing separate systems~\cite{calich2025hdis}. Some permanent housing programs for veterans are also captured in distinct data systems~\cite{calich2025hdis}. Consequently, the data likely underestimates the true size of the homeless population, particularly unsheltered individuals who do not access formal services~\cite{calich2025hdis, culhane2024learning}. This introduces a potential for bias: CoCs that are more effective at engaging and bringing unsheltered individuals into their service systems might, paradoxically, appear to have higher homelessness counts in the dataset, even if their overall performance in reducing homelessness is strong. This nuance must be carefully considered in the interpretation of ``effectiveness" and acknowledged as a limitation in the discussion of findings.

\subsection{Census Population Data: Sources for County and City-Level Information}

Population data, essential for contextualizing homelessness counts and calculating rates, was primarily sourced from the U.S. Census Bureau, accessible through \url{data.census.gov}~\cite{censusgov}. The Census Bureau provides statistics at various granularities, including all states, counties, and cities or towns with populations of 5,000 or more~\cite{censusgov}. This multi-level granularity is critical for aligning population figures with CoC boundaries.

The data utilized included information from the American Community Survey (ACS), specifically 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year estimates, which provide detailed social, economic, demographic, and housing characteristics down to the block-group level~\cite{censusgov}. Decennial Census data was also incorporated for its high-resolution population counts by sex, age, race, and Hispanic origin~\cite{censusgov}. To ensure temporal consistency and account for population changes between decennial counts, Population Estimates and Projections were consulted~\cite{censusgov}. Data was accessed via the interactive tables and mapping tools on the \url{data.census.gov} platform~\cite{censusgov}.

A challenge in this analysis was reconciling homelessness data from CoCs with county and city census data. Some CoCs cover multiple counties, while others subdivide a single county. In particular, city-level data was required for accurately calculating population figures for CoCs within larger counties like Los Angeles.

\subsection{Geographical Data: County and CoC Boundaries, Lat/Long, and Adjacency}

To incorporate spatial information into the analysis, several geographical datasets were utilized. County boundaries were obtained from the California County Boundaries and Identifiers dataset available on \url{data.ca.gov}~\cite{caopendatacountyb}. Geographic boundaries for Continuums of Care were obtained from the HUD Exchange CoC GIS Tools~\cite{hudexchange2024gis}.

To represent the geographical location of each CoC, its centroid (latitude and longitude) was calculated. This involved using the CoC's polygon shapefile to determine its geometric center of mass~\cite{mygeodesycentroid}. This method provides a more accurate representation of a CoC's central point compared to simply averaging county centroids, especially for irregularly shaped CoCs or those spanning disparate areas~\cite{mygeodesycentroid}. For CoCs encompassing multiple counties or cities, the centroid of the aggregated CoC polygon was computed.

An adjacency graph of CoCs was constructed to account for spatial relationships. A baseline for regional connectivity was established using the U.S. Census Bureau's ``County Adjacency File," which provides a national list of neighboring counties, including those whose boundaries meet within bodies of water~\cite{census2010adjacency}. Building upon this, a CoC-level adjacency graph was constructed by identifying CoCs that share common boundaries through spatial analysis of the downloaded CoC GIS shapefiles~\cite{hudexchange2024gis}. This captures direct spatial relationships between CoCs. The inclusion of geographical data, particularly CoC centroids and adjacency, extends beyond mere visualization; it aims to understand the spatial dynamics of homelessness. Neighboring CoCs may experience shared challenges, such as economic downturns or housing market pressures, or exhibit ``spillover effects" from policies or interventions. For instance, successful prevention efforts in one CoC might reduce inflow to adjacent areas, or conversely, restrictive policies could displace individuals to neighboring CoCs. Incorporating an adjacency graph allows the models to account for spatial autocorrelation, providing a more realistic and comprehensive understanding of CoC performance within a regional context.

\subsection{Data Normalization and Preprocessing}

\subsubsection{Reconciling CoC and County Geographies}
A primary challenge involved the differing geographical organization of homelessness data (by CoCs) and census population data (primarily by county or city). CoC boundaries do not always align perfectly with county lines; some CoCs encompass multiple counties, while some large counties are split into several CoCs. To address this, CoC GIS Shapefiles from HUD Exchange~\cite{hudexchange2024gis} were used in conjunction with California County Boundaries shapefiles~\cite{caopendatacountyb, censusgov}. For CoCs that span multiple entire counties (e.g., CA-516, which covers Redding/Shasta, Siskiyou, Lassen, Plumas, Del Norte, Modoc, and Sierra Counties~\cite{caopendatahomelessness}), the census population data for all constituent counties were aggregated to represent the total population within that CoC.

\subsubsection{Addressing Multi-CoC Jurisdictions (e.g., Los Angeles County)}
Los Angeles County, a major population center with over 10 million residents~\cite{censusgov}, is a critical example of a jurisdiction split into multiple CoCs. Relying solely on county-level census data for these CoCs would introduce significant inaccuracies. To overcome this, city-level population data was obtained from the U.S. Census Bureau (\url{data.census.gov}, QuickFacts)~\cite{censusgov}. Los Angeles City alone accounts for nearly 3.9 million residents~\cite{censusgov}, and the "Greater Los Angeles" CoC reports a total homeless population exceeding 75,000~\cite{caopendatahomelessness}, underscoring the necessity for sub-county granularity. Specific cities and their populations were meticulously mapped to their corresponding CoCs within Los Angeles County based on official CoC boundary definitions. For CoCs that cover specific sets of cities, their populations were summed. For CoCs that include unincorporated areas or portions of cities, more advanced spatial disaggregation techniques, potentially utilizing block-group level data from the American Community Survey (ACS 5-year estimates available in the HUD CoC GIS Toolkit~\cite{hudexchange2024gis}), were employed to estimate the population within the precise CoC boundaries.

\subsubsection{Constructing Geographical Features (Centroids, Adjacency Matrix)}
The geometric centroid (latitude and longitude) for each CoC was calculated. This involved using the CoC's polygon shapefile to determine its center of mass~\cite{mygeodesycentroid}, a more accurate approach than simple bounding box midpoints, especially for complex geometries~\cite{mygeodesycentroid}. A binary adjacency matrix was constructed for all California CoCs, indicating whether any two CoCs share a common border. This was derived directly from the spatial analysis of the CoC GIS shapefiles~\cite{hudexchange2024gis}. This matrix serves as a feature in the statistical models to account for spatial dependencies.

The detailed data normalization process, particularly the reconciliation of CoC and county/city geographies, represents a critical technical and analytical undertaking. The chosen aggregation method directly impacts the accuracy of the population denominator for each CoC, which is essential for calculating per-capita homelessness rates and, consequently, for fairly comparing CoC effectiveness. Inaccurate population figures could lead to misleading "effectiveness" rankings. This meticulous preprocessing reinforces the "rigorous statistical analysis" aspect of the study's novelty and ensures the reliability of the subsequent model outputs.

\section{Analysis}

This section details the statistical modeling framework employed, including the rationale for selecting Random Forest and ANCOVA, the feature engineering process, and the methods used to interpret model insights and evaluate CoC effectiveness.

\subsection{Statistical Modeling Framework: Random Forest and ANCOVA}

\subsubsection{Theoretical Underpinnings and Assumptions}
The analysis utilized two distinct statistical modeling approaches: Random Forest and Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA).

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Random Forest:} This is an ensemble learning method that constructs a multitude of decision trees during its training phase and aggregates their outputs to produce a final prediction (for regression tasks, this is typically the mean prediction)~\cite{ibm2025randomforest}. A key advantage of Random Forest is its non-parametric nature, which means it does not impose strong assumptions about the underlying data distribution or the precise functional form of relationships between variables. This characteristic makes it robust to non-linear patterns, complex interactions, and multicollinearity, which are frequently encountered in social science datasets where relationships can be intricate~\cite{ibm2025randomforest}. The model's efficiency and inherent capacity for assessing feature importance further contribute to its utility~\cite{ibm2025randomforest}.

    \item \textbf{ANCOVA (Analysis of Covariance):} ANCOVA is a statistical technique that blends elements of Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) and linear regression~\cite{field2013discovering}. Its primary objective is to enhance the precision of detecting effects on a dependent variable (in this case, the homelessness count) by statistically adjusting for the influence of one or more continuous concomitant variables, or covariates (such as population size)~\cite{field2013discovering}. Standard ANCOVA models operate under several assumptions: the dependent variable and the covariate are expected to follow a bivariate normal distribution within each group, and the variance-covariance matrix of these distributions should be identical across groups. A crucial assumption is the homogeneity of regression slopes, implying that the relationship (slope) between the dependent variable and the covariate is consistent across all groups (CoCs)~\cite{realstatsancova, aatbioquestancova}. By controlling for the covariate, ANCOVA effectively reduces the error variance, thereby increasing the precision of tests for differences between CoCs~\cite{field2013discovering}. It also yields adjusted group means, allowing for more equitable comparisons of CoCs that are not biased by inherent differences in the covariate, such as varying population sizes~\cite{field2013discovering}.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Rationale for Model Selection and Comparative Application}
The selection and comparative application of both Random Forest and ANCOVA were driven by the objective of achieving a robust and multi-faceted assessment of CoC effectiveness.

Random Forest was chosen for its exceptional ability to model complex, potentially non-linear relationships and interactions among numerous features without requiring explicit specification of these relationships. This capability is particularly valuable in understanding social phenomena like homelessness, where multiple factors interact in intricate and often non-obvious ways. Its built-in feature importance measures also provide valuable insights into which predictors exert the most influence on the outcome~\cite{ibm2025randomforest, numberanalytics2025importance}.

ANCOVA was selected as a complementary linear model to provide a more traditional, statistically interpretable baseline. ANCOVA allows for direct interpretation of the coefficients (slopes and intercepts), which indicate the linear impact of specific covariates on homelessness rates after accounting for population~\cite{field2013discovering}. This offers a clear, controlled view of individual variable effects, which can be highly relevant for informing policy design.

The comparative analysis, employing both Random Forest and ANCOVA, enables a comprehensive and nuanced understanding. Random Forest can identify overall predictive power and feature importance, capturing complex patterns that a purely linear ANCOVA model might miss. Conversely, ANCOVA offers direct, interpretable insights into linear relationships and the controlled impact of specific covariates. This dual approach leverages the strengths of each model, providing both broad predictive insights and specific, controlled linear relationships. This enhances the overall interpretability of the findings and strengthens their actionable policy implications. This strategic choice of models is driven by a desire for comprehensive understanding. While Random Forest is a powerful predictive tool, it can sometimes be perceived as a "black box"~\cite{molnar2022interpretable}. However, its feature importance metrics provide global perspectives on the general drivers of homelessness. ANCOVA, as a linear model, offers direct coefficient interpretation, allowing for a more granular understanding of how specific variables linearly influence homelessness after controlling for population. This combined approach provides both broad predictive insights and specific, controlled linear relationships, thereby enhancing the overall understanding and practical utility of the results.

\subsection{Feature Engineering: Population vs. Log-Transformed Population}

The dependent variable, homelessness counts, represents count data, which often exhibits a skewed distribution. Population size is a fundamental predictor of homelessness, but the relationship between population and homelessness count may not be strictly linear. To address potential issues such as non-linearity, skewed distributions, and unequal variance in residuals (heteroscedasticity), the application of a natural logarithm transformation to the population variable was explored~\cite{hospitality2024log}. This transformation compresses larger values and expands smaller ones, which can help achieve a more symmetrical distribution and make the data more suitable for linear modeling assumptions, particularly for ANCOVA, and potentially improve the performance of Random Forest by normalizing feature scales~\cite{hospitality2024log}.

Both statistical models, Random Forest and ANCOVA, were trained and evaluated using both the raw population count and its log-transformed counterpart as a feature. The decision between the two was based on model performance metrics, such as lower Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) and higher $R^2$ values, and for ANCOVA, on the satisfaction of linear regression assumptions, including normality of residuals and homoscedasticity~\cite{hospitality2024log}. This systematic testing of both raw and log-transformed population variables is a hallmark of rigorous statistical methodology. Homelessness data, being counts, is inherently prone to positive skewness, which can violate the assumptions of linear models like ANCOVA. Log transformation is a standard technique to mitigate these issues, leading to more reliable coefficient estimates and valid statistical inferences. For Random Forest, while less sensitive to distribution, normalizing feature scales through log transformation can still enhance model performance and stability. This methodological choice demonstrates a deep understanding of data characteristics and their impact on model validity and robustness.

\subsection{Interpreting Model Insights}

\subsubsection{Random Forest Feature Importance}
To understand the relative influence of different predictors on older adult homelessness, feature importance was assessed using two primary methods inherent to Random Forests:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gini Importance (Mean Decrease in Impurity):} This metric quantifies how much the impurity (or randomness) within a node of a decision tree decreases when a specific feature is used to split the data. The sum of these decreases across all trees in the forest, normalized, yields the Gini importance score for each feature~\cite{ibm2025randomforest, numberanalytics2025importance}. Features that consistently lead to greater impurity reduction are assigned higher scores, indicating their greater predictive power.
    \item \textbf{Permutation Feature Importance (Mean Decrease in Accuracy/MSE):} This method assesses the significance of each feature by randomly shuffling its values in the out-of-bag sample (data not used during tree construction) and measuring the resulting decrease in model prediction accuracy (or increase in Mean Squared Error for regression)~\cite{ibm2025randomforest, numberanalytics2025importance}. This approach provides a robust measure of a feature's independent contribution to model performance, as shuffling a truly important feature will significantly degrade predictions~\cite{ibm2025randomforest}.
\end{itemize}

Features with high importance scores are considered key drivers of the outcome (homelessness count)~\cite{numberanalytics2025importance}. This analysis provides a global understanding of which predictors are generally most influential in predicting older adult homelessness across CoCs.

\subsubsection{ANCOVA Coefficient Analysis}
For the ANCOVA model, the estimated coefficients (slopes) for each covariate were interpreted. A coefficient represents the average change in the dependent variable (homelessness count) for a one-unit increase in the corresponding independent variable, while holding all other variables in the model constant~\cite{field2013discovering}. The intercept represents the adjusted mean of the dependent variable when all predictors are zero, though its direct meaning can vary depending on predictor scaling~\cite{field2013discovering}. Statistical significance ($p$-values) associated with each coefficient indicated the reliability and robustness of the observed relationship. It is crucial to acknowledge that regression coefficients are "marginal results," reflecting the unique effect of a predictor after controlling for other variables in the model~\cite{field2013discovering}. Their values and significance can change depending on the other variables included, especially in the presence of correlated predictors~\cite{field2013discovering}.

\subsubsection{Synthesis of Findings Across Models}
A comprehensive synthesis of the insights gained from both models was conducted. This involved discussing common themes in feature importance and coefficient significance. Any discrepancies were carefully examined; for instance, if a feature showed high importance in Random Forest (suggesting complex interactions or non-linear effects) but had a non-significant or small linear coefficient in ANCOVA (possibly due to linearity assumptions or multicollinearity)~\cite{field2013discovering}. This multi-model perspective offers a richer, more nuanced understanding of the complex factors driving older adult homelessness. Employing both Gini and Permutation Importance for Random Forest provides a more robust and comprehensive view of feature influence, as each method has different strengths and potential biases. Comparing these insights with the direct coefficients from ANCOVA allows for differentiation between features that exert a strong, potentially non-linear or interactive effect (more evident in Random Forest importance) and those with a clear, linear, and controlled impact (ANCOVA). This multi-faceted interpretation strategy provides a richer, more nuanced understanding of the complex factors driving homelessness, helping to avoid oversimplified or misleading conclusions about feature importance.

The identified significant predictors were then related to existing academic literature and policy discussions on homelessness risk factors, such as housing affordability, economic conditions, health vulnerabilities, and racial disparities~\cite{terner2023older, huduser2019market, culhane2024learning}. For example, if "median rent" consistently ranks high in Random Forest importance and also exhibits a statistically significant positive coefficient in ANCOVA, it provides strong evidence that higher rental costs are a direct and substantial contributor to increased homelessness, aligning with existing research~\cite{ppic2025homelessness, huduser2019market, culhane2024learning}. Conversely, if a geographical feature (e.g., CoC latitude) is highly important in Random Forest but shows no significant linear effect in ANCOVA, it suggests that its influence might be mediated by other factors or involve complex, non-linear spatial patterns, prompting further qualitative or spatial econometric investigation. This multi-model approach enables a more nuanced understanding of the complex factors driving homelessness.

\subsection{Evaluating CoC Effectiveness: The Prediction Set Approach}

\subsubsection{Methodology for Generating Standardized Predictions}
To fairly compare the effectiveness of different CoCs, it is essential to account for confounding variables that are largely outside a CoC's direct control, such as overall population size, regional economic conditions, and housing market dynamics. The "prediction set" approach addresses this by creating a hypothetical scenario where these confounding variables are standardized across all CoCs.

The process involved identifying key confounding variables based on feature importance analysis and existing literature (e.g., total population, median income, housing costs, regional unemployment rates, drawing insights from~\cite{ppic2025homelessness, huduser2019market, culhane2024learning}). For each CoC, a "prediction set" was constructed by replacing its actual values for these identified confounding variables with a standardized value, such as the statewide average for that variable, or the average of CoCs within a similar urban/rural classification. Crucially, CoC-specific characteristics that reflect their programmatic efforts or fixed attributes (e.g., geographical location, adjacency to other CoCs) were kept at their actual values. The trained statistical models (Random Forest and ANCOVA) were then applied to this standardized prediction set for each CoC to generate a "predicted homelessness count" under these controlled, "average" external conditions.

\subsubsection{Ranking CoCs Based on Predicted Homelessness Outcomes}
By comparing the predicted homelessness counts under these standardized conditions, CoCs with lower predicted counts (relative to their actual performance or other CoCs) can be interpreted as being "more effective" at managing homelessness, given similar external circumstances. Conversely, higher predicted counts might indicate "less effectiveness" or greater challenges despite similar external contexts. This ranking provides a more equitable and insightful comparison of CoC performance, as it attempts to isolate the impact of their local strategies and interventions from the influence of broader socioeconomic and demographic factors. This can inform targeted resource allocation, facilitate the identification and dissemination of best practices from high-performing CoCs, and guide tailored interventions for CoCs facing greater challenges.

This "prediction set" method serves as a sophisticated form of counterfactual analysis, allowing for the exploration of "what if" scenarios where a CoC operated under a set of average conditions~\cite{microsoft2025counterfactuals}. This moves beyond mere predictive accuracy to provide actionable insights for policy. It enables the simulation of what would happen if a given CoC operated under a standardized set of external conditions (e.g., average population, average housing costs). This is profoundly valuable for policy evaluation because it attempts to isolate the "treatment effect" of a CoC's unique strategies and interventions from exogenous factors that are beyond their control. This provides a more robust, equitable, and actionable basis for comparing CoC effectiveness, directly addressing the core objective of identifying "which CoC are more and less effective." Furthermore, by making the "why" behind the ranking transparent, it enhances the interpretability of the models for policymakers, a key tenet of interpretable machine learning in social policy~\cite{molnar2022interpretable} and aligning with the mission to apply AI for social good~\cite{realgoodai}.

Conversely, if both models performed similarly, it might indicate that linear relationships are dominant. This rigorous evaluation strengthens the overall credibility and the "rigorous statistical analysis" aspect of the research.

The impact of using raw population versus log-transformed population on model performance for both Random Forest and ANCOVA was also analyzed. The log transformation, which compresses larger values and spreads smaller ones, was evaluated for its effectiveness in improving model fit, reducing residual skewness, and addressing heteroscedasticity, particularly for the ANCOVA model~\cite{hospitality2024log}. The results showed that for ANCOVA, the log transformation generally improved the satisfaction of linear regression assumptions and led to more robust coefficient estimates. For Random Forest, while less sensitive to distributional assumptions, the transformation sometimes contributed to marginal improvements in performance or stability.

A K-fold cross-validation strategy was employed to ensure the robustness and generalizability of the model performance estimates. This method involved partitioning the data into K subsets, training the model on K-1 subsets, and evaluating it on the held-out subset, repeating this process K times. \subsection{Policy Implications and Recommendations}

The findings from this data-driven assessment of CoC effectiveness carry significant policy implications for state and local governments, as well as CoC leadership in California. The analysis yields concrete, actionable recommendations aimed at mitigating and ultimately reducing homelessness among older adults.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Address Systemic Housing and Economic Factors:} The consistent emergence of housing affordability (e.g., median rent) and economic stability (e.g., unemployment rates) as significant predictors across both statistical models underscores the critical need to address these systemic factors as fundamental strategies to combat older adult homelessness~\cite{ppic2025homelessness, huduser2019market, terner2023older}. Policy efforts should prioritize increasing the supply of affordable housing, implementing rent stabilization measures, and expanding rental assistance programs to prevent older adults from falling into homelessness due to economic precarity.

    \item \textbf{Tailored Interventions for the 65+ Age Group:} The unique vulnerabilities of older adults, including their increased health challenges, financial precarity, and the prevalence of late-onset homelessness, necessitate tailored interventions~\cite{terner2023older}. Recommendations include:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Expanded Eviction Prevention Programs:} Given that financial factors often precede homelessness for this group, robust and accessible eviction prevention services, including legal aid and emergency financial assistance, are crucial.
        \item \textbf{Accessible Supportive Housing:} Development of housing models that integrate age-appropriate supportive services, addressing health, mobility, and social isolation needs.
        \item \textbf{Streamlined Benefit Access:} Efforts to connect older adults to all eligible benefits (e.g., Social Security, SSI, Medicare/Medicaid) to stabilize their income and healthcare access.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Leverage Data-Driven Decision-Making and Predictive Analytics:} The study demonstrates the power of combining public data with rigorous statistical analysis to derive actionable insights. State and local governments, and CoCs, should continue to invest in and refine data-driven decision-making processes and predictive analytics for early intervention and targeted prevention efforts. Successful models, such as the California Policy Lab's Homelessness Prevention Unit, which uses predictive modeling to identify and proactively reach out to high-risk individuals, offer a blueprint for effective, preventative strategies~\cite{capolicylab2025hpu}.

    \item \textbf{Facilitate Best Practice Sharing and Knowledge Transfer:} The identified high-performing CoCs, whose actual homelessness counts were lower than predicted under standardized conditions, represent valuable sources of knowledge. Mechanisms should be established to facilitate the sharing of their successful strategies, program designs, and operational efficiencies across the state. This could include regular forums, detailed case studies, and funding for peer-to-peer learning initiatives.

    \item \textbf{Enhance Accountability for Homelessness Program Funds:} Aligning with previous audit findings~\cite{ppic2025homelessness}, there is a clear need for increased accountability measures for homelessness program funds. The state should implement robust, standardized evaluation frameworks that track outcomes at the CoC level, ensuring that investments translate into measurable reductions in homelessness. This study’s methodology offers a potential framework for such evaluations. This section serves as the crucial bridge between academic research and practical policy. The recommendations are specific, actionable, and directly derived from the empirical findings, demonstrating the practical application of the mission to apply advanced analytical methods for positive social change.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Study Limitations}

Despite the rigorous methodology employed, this study has several limitations that warrant acknowledgment.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Data Limitations:} The primary homelessness data, derived from the Homelessness Data Integration System (HDIS), relies on individuals accessing services within CoCs~\cite{caopendatahomelessness}. This inherently leads to a potential undercounting of unsheltered individuals who do not engage with formal service systems~\cite{culhane2024learning, caopendatahomelessness}. Consequently, the reported homelessness counts may not fully represent the true scale of the problem, and CoCs that are more effective at outreach and engagement might appear to have higher counts due to better data capture. Specific CoC data gaps, such as the absence of data for Tehama and Lake Counties, and limitations for Yuba/Sutter and Alpine/Inyo/Mono CoCs, also affect the comprehensiveness of the dataset~\cite{caopendatahomelessness}. The reliance on reported service data might influence the representativeness of the homeless population captured, potentially skewing the understanding of "effectiveness." Explicitly stating these limitations is paramount for maintaining scientific integrity and credibility, managing reader expectations regarding the generalizability and certainty of the findings.

    \item \textbf{Model Limitations:} While both Random Forest and ANCOVA offer distinct advantages, they also have limitations. ANCOVA relies on assumptions such as homogeneity of regression slopes and normality of residuals, which, despite efforts at log transformation, may not be perfectly met across all CoCs~\cite{realstatsancova, aatbioquestancova}. Random Forest, while powerful, can sometimes be perceived as a "black box" model, making the direct causal interpretation of individual feature effects challenging, even with feature importance metrics~\cite{molnar2022interpretable}. The study's efforts to enhance interpretability through the prediction set approach and comparative analysis mitigate, but do not entirely eliminate, this challenge.

    \item \textbf{Causation vs. Correlation:} Even with the "prediction set" approach, which aims to approximate a controlled comparison by standardizing confounding variables, the study primarily identifies correlations rather than definitive causal relationships. While the methodology allows for a more equitable comparison of CoC performance by isolating the impact of local strategies from exogenous factors, it does not fully control for all unobserved variables or complex feedback loops inherent in social systems~\cite{molnar2022interpretable}.

    \item \textbf{Geographical Data Simplification:} The representation of complex spatial patterns through simple CoC centroids (latitude/longitude) and a binary adjacency matrix is a simplification. While valuable, it may not fully capture the nuances of geographical influence, such as varying population densities within CoCs, or the strength of connectivity between neighboring areas (e.g., shared transportation networks, economic ties). More sophisticated spatial econometric models could further refine this aspect.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future Research Directions}

Building upon the findings and limitations of this study, several avenues for future research emerge:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Incorporation of More Granular Data:} Future research could explore the integration of even more granular data, such as block-group level ACS data, to refine population estimates for CoCs, particularly for those that subdivide counties or include unincorporated areas. This would enhance the precision of population denominators and improve the accuracy of per-capita homelessness rates.
    \item \textbf{Dynamic Analysis and Time-Series Modeling:} Extending the current cross-sectional analysis to a longitudinal study, incorporating time-series data on homelessness counts and relevant covariates, would enable the investigation of trends, the impact of policy changes over time, and the dynamic effectiveness of CoCs. This could involve panel data models or more advanced time-series forecasting techniques.
    \item \textbf{Qualitative and Mixed-Methods Research:} To complement the quantitative findings, future research could incorporate qualitative methods, such as in-depth interviews with CoC leaders, service providers, and individuals experiencing homelessness. This would provide richer contextual understanding of the factors driving effectiveness or challenges, explaining the "why" behind the statistical patterns.
    \item \textbf{Exploration of Additional Predictors:} Expanding the set of predictor variables to include more detailed information on CoC funding allocations, specific program types and capacities (e.g., number of rapid re-housing units, prevention slots), local housing policies (e.g., zoning laws, rent control), and healthcare access could provide a more comprehensive understanding of CoC performance.
    \item \textbf{Advanced Spatial Econometrics:} To more fully account for spatial dependencies and spillover effects, future work could employ advanced spatial econometric models that explicitly model spatial autocorrelation in the error terms or the dependent variable, providing a more nuanced understanding of how neighboring CoCs influence each other.
    \item \textbf{Causal Inference Techniques:} Exploring advanced causal inference techniques, such as difference-in-differences or instrumental variables, where appropriate data allows, could help move beyond correlation to establish more definitive causal links between CoC interventions and homelessness outcomes.
    \item \textbf{Bias Detection and Fairness in AI Models:} Further research into the interpretability of machine learning models for social policy, focusing on detecting and mitigating biases within the data and model predictions, would be crucial. This aligns with the principles of ethical AI development and ensures that policy recommendations do not inadvertently perpetuate existing inequities.
\end{enumerate}
