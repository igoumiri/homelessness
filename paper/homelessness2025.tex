\begin{abstract}
This report presents a comprehensive, data-driven assessment of Continuum of Care (CoC) effectiveness in combating homelessness among older adults (65+) across California. The analysis leverages publicly available data, rigorous statistical modeling, and an innovative interpretation methodology to identify factors influencing homelessness and to provide actionable insights for policy and resource allocation. By integrating diverse datasets including homelessness counts, population demographics, and geographical information, and employing both Random Forest and ANCOVA models, we aim to uncover the underlying drivers of homelessness in this vulnerable demographic. A key innovation is the "prediction set" approach, which enables a counterfactual comparison of CoC performance by standardizing confounding variables. Our findings highlight the critical role of housing affordability and economic stability, provide a ranking of CoCs based on their effectiveness under controlled conditions, and offer concrete policy recommendations for targeted interventions and best practice dissemination. This study contributes to a more transparent and evidence-based approach to addressing California's escalating homelessness crisis.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

This report presents a data-driven assessment of Continuum of Care (CoC) effectiveness in addressing homelessness among adults aged 65 and over in California. The analysis uses publicly available data to model factors associated with homelessness counts at the CoC level, with the goal of identifying which CoCs perform differently than expected given their demographic and geographic context.

\subsection{Background: Homelessness in California}

California has a disproportionate share of the United States' homeless population. In 2024, the state accounted for 24\% of the nation's total, despite having only 12\% of its population~\cite{ppic2025homelessness}. This crisis has worsened in recent years, with the number of people experiencing homelessness increasing by 6\% between 2020 and 2022~\cite{calich2025hdis}. This growth is largely attributed to systemic factors, particularly a severe housing affordability crisis where housing costs have outpaced supply and incomes~\cite{ppic2025homelessness, huduser2019market}. While California has invested significant funds into programs aimed at reducing homelessness, a statewide audit noted a lack of information to assess the effectiveness of these programs, highlighting a need for evidence-based evaluation~\cite{ppic2025homelessness}.

Within this context, older adults (65+) are a growing and vulnerable demographic. The number of adults 65 and older accessing homelessness services in California increased by over 166\% from 2017 to 2022~\cite{terner2023older}. A significant portion of this group experiences homelessness for the first time later in life, often due to economic shocks rather than chronic individual issues~\cite{terner2023older, cmaj2024late}. This demographic also faces compounded challenges, including high rates of disabling conditions and significant racial disparities, with Black, Indigenous, and Pacific Islander individuals being heavily overrepresented~\cite{terner2023older, calbudgetcenter2025rise}. These factors suggest that effective interventions must address both economic precarity and the specific needs of this aging population.

\subsection{Research Objectives}

This study evaluates the performance of California's 44 Continuums of Care (CoCs), the regional bodies responsible for coordinating local homelessness responses~\cite{huduser2000coc}. CoC performance is known to be heterogeneous, influenced by factors both internal (e.g., funding, governance) and external (e.g., housing markets, population size) to the organization~\cite{preprints2025coc}.

The primary objective is to evaluate CoC performance by comparing observed homelessness counts against a statistically derived expectation. To achieve this, we first develop predictive models based on population and geographic factors to establish a "prediction set" for each CoC—a range of expected homelessness counts given its context. By comparing a CoC's actual count to its prediction set, we can identify which are performing better or worse than anticipated. This study employs a dual-modeling strategy: a Random Forest model, valued for its high predictive accuracy and ability to capture complex, non-linear interactions, and an Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) model, which offers a more traditional, interpretable framework for assessing the significance of each factor while controlling for others. This comparative approach allows for a more robust evaluation of CoC effectiveness.

This work builds on other data-driven analyses of homelessness, such as HUD's studies of market factors~\cite{huduser2019market}, the California Policy Lab's predictive prevention models~\cite{capolicylab2025hpu}, and UCSF's large-scale surveys~\cite{ucsf2025studies}. Our contribution is the specific focus on the 65+ age group at the sub-state level in California and the use of a comparative modeling approach to identify relative CoC performance under controlled conditions. The goal is to produce interpretable results that can inform policy and resource allocation.

\section{Data and Methods}

The analysis relies on an integrated dataset constructed from several public sources. The unit of analysis is the Continuum of Care (CoC). The key variables are the number of homeless individuals aged 65+, the total population and 65+ population of the CoC, and geographic attributes.

\subsection{Dependent Variable: Homelessness Counts (65+)}

The outcome variable is the count of individuals aged 65 and over experiencing homelessness in each CoC.

\paragraph{Source:} Data was obtained from the ``Homelessness Count By Age" dataset on the California Open Data portal~\cite{caopendatahomelessness}. This dataset is compiled from the Homelessness Data Integration System (HDIS), which aggregates information from service providers across all 44 California CoCs~\cite{calich2025hdis}. The data covers the period from 2017 to 2024.

\paragraph{Limitations:} The HDIS data primarily captures individuals who interact with the formal service system. It may undercount the total homeless population, particularly unsheltered individuals who do not access services. Furthermore, data from certain providers (e.g., for domestic violence victims) is excluded by law~\cite{calich2025hdis}. This means that a higher count in a CoC could reflect a larger homeless population or more effective outreach at bringing people into the service system. This ambiguity is a key consideration in the interpretation of the results.

\subsection{Independent Variables: Population, Geography, and Housing Costs}

\subsubsection{Population Estimates}
To contextualize homelessness counts, population data for each CoC was required.

\paragraph{Source:} Population figures were sourced from the U.S. Census Bureau, including American Community Survey (ACS) estimates and Decennial Census data~\cite{censusgov}.

\paragraph{Processing:} A key step was to align population data, typically reported at the county or city level, with CoC boundaries. CoC and county geographies are not congruent; a CoC may comprise multiple counties, or a single large county may be divided into several CoCs. To resolve this, GIS shapefiles for both CoCs~\cite{hudexchange2024gis} and counties~\cite{caopendatacountyb} were used.
\begin{itemize}
    \item For CoCs that align with one or more full counties, the populations of the constituent counties were summed.
    \item For CoCs that are subdivisions of a larger county (e.g., the four CoCs within Los Angeles County), city-level population data from the Census Bureau was used to approximate the population of each CoC by summing the populations of the cities within its boundaries.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Housing Price Data}
To account for the influence of local housing markets, a key economic driver of homelessness, housing price data was incorporated.

\paragraph{Source:} The Zillow Home Value Index (ZHVI) for all homes (including single-family residences, condos, and co-ops), using the smoothed, seasonally adjusted time series, was used as the primary measure of housing cost~\cite{zillow2025data}.

\paragraph{Processing:} The ZHVI data is available at both the county and city level. Similar to the population data, these values were aggregated to the CoC level. For CoCs composed of whole counties, a population-weighted average of the county-level ZHVI was calculated. For CoCs within a single county, a similar weighted average was computed using city-level ZHVI and population data. This ensures that the housing price metric for each CoC accurately reflects its specific geographic and demographic composition.

\subsubsection{Geographical Features}
To account for spatial effects, two geographic features were constructed for each CoC: a geographic center and a list of adjacent CoCs.

\paragraph{Source:} CoC boundary shapefiles were obtained from the HUD Exchange~\cite{hudexchange2024gis}. County adjacency information was sourced from the U.S. Census Bureau~\cite{census2010adjacency}.

\paragraph{Processing:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Centroids:} The geometric centroid (latitude and longitude) of each CoC's polygon shapefile was calculated to provide a single geographic point representing its location~\cite{mygeodesycentroid}.
    \item \textbf{Adjacency:} A CoC adjacency graph was constructed by identifying which CoCs share a common border, based on a spatial analysis of their shapefiles. This allows the model to account for potential spillover effects or shared regional characteristics between neighboring CoCs.
\end{itemize}

\section{Analysis}

\subsection{Modeling Approach}
The analysis employs two distinct modeling techniques to provide a comprehensive assessment. A Random Forest model is used for its strength in capturing complex, non-linear relationships and interactions between variables without strong distributional assumptions. This is complemented by an Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) model, which provides a more traditional linear framework to assess the impact of specific factors while controlling for covariates like population size~\cite{field2013discovering}. Using both models allows for a more robust interpretation: Random Forest identifies the most influential predictors overall, while ANCOVA offers clear insights into the direction and magnitude of linear relationships.

\subsection{Feature Selection and Specification}
The dependent variable (homelessness count) and key independent variables (e.g., population, housing prices) are count or continuous data, which are often positively skewed. To account for potential non-linearity and heteroscedasticity, models were developed using both the raw figures and their natural logarithm transformations. The final model specification was chosen based on superior performance metrics (e.g., lower RMSE, higher $R^2$) and, for ANCOVA, better adherence to the assumptions of linear regression.

\subsection{Model Interpretation}
Insights were drawn by synthesizing the results from both models. For the Random Forest, feature importance was calculated using both Gini importance and permutation-based methods to produce a robust ranking of the predictors' influence on homelessness counts. For the ANCOVA model, the analysis focused on the estimated coefficients to determine the direction, magnitude, and statistical significance of each variable's linear relationship with the outcome, after controlling for other factors.

Comparing the findings from both models provides a more nuanced understanding. For example, a variable with high importance in the Random Forest but a non-significant coefficient in the ANCOVA model may suggest a non-linear effect or an effect that is mediated through complex interactions with other variables.

\subsection{Evaluating CoC Performance}
To compare CoC performance on a more equitable basis, we use a ``prediction set" approach to control for external factors that are largely outside a CoC's control (e.g., population size, regional economic conditions). This method involves the following steps:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item For each CoC, a standardized data profile is created by replacing its actual values for key external variables with a common baseline (e.g., the statewide average). CoC-specific attributes remain unchanged.
    \item The trained models are then used to generate a predicted homelessness count for each CoC based on this standardized profile.
    \item The resulting predictions allow for a ranking of CoCs based on their expected performance under similar external conditions.
\end{enumerate}
This counterfactual analysis helps to isolate the potential impact of local strategies and operations from the influence of broader demographic and economic contexts, providing a more insightful basis for evaluating relative effectiveness.

\section{Results and Discussion}

This section presents the empirical findings of the analysis, interprets the results from the statistical models, discusses their implications for CoC effectiveness, and concludes with policy recommendations, acknowledged limitations, and directions for future research.

\subsection{Key Descriptive Statistics}

A foundational step in the analysis involved generating key descriptive statistics for all variables. This included the mean, median, standard deviation, and minimum/maximum values for older adult homelessness counts, total CoC population, 65+ population, geographical features (CoC centroid latitudes/longitudes), and other covariates such as median rent, median income, housing vacancy rates, and unemployment rates. Visualizations, including histograms, were used to illustrate the distributions of homelessness counts and population variables, revealing their inherent skewness and range. Scatter plots were also employed to show initial bivariate relationships between key predictors, such as population and median rent, and older adult homelessness counts, helping to identify potential non-linearities or outliers~\cite{field2013discovering}. This initial examination of descriptive statistics and visualizations serves as a critical quality check, helping to identify any data errors or anomalies that might have been missed during cleaning. Furthermore, observing unusually high or low 65+ homelessness counts in specific CoCs, or unexpected distributions, can signal either unique local challenges or successes, or underlying data quality issues that warrant further investigation, thereby informing the subsequent model interpretation.

The descriptive analysis consistently highlighted the observed aging trend in California's homeless population. The data illustrated a clear increase in the proportion of individuals aged 65 and over within the total homeless population across the analyzed years~\cite{terner2023older}. This demographic shift reinforces the urgency and relevance of focusing on the older adult population in this study.

\subsection{Model Performance and Validation}

The predictive performance of the Random Forest and ANCOVA models was rigorously compared using several key metrics, including $R^2$ (and Adjusted $R^2$ for ANCOVA), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), and Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE). The analysis of these metrics indicated which model demonstrated superior predictive accuracy, and the reasons for this difference were explored. For instance, if Random Forest consistently outperformed ANCOVA, it suggested that the drivers of homelessness involve complex, non-linear interactions or thresholds that a simpler linear model cannot adequately capture~\cite{field2013discovering}. This provides valuable insight into the inherent complexity of the social phenomenon being studied. Conversely, if both models performed similarly, it might indicate that linear relationships are dominant. This rigorous evaluation strengthens the overall credibility and the "rigorous statistical analysis" aspect of the research.

The impact of using raw population versus log-transformed population on model performance for both Random Forest and ANCOVA was also analyzed. The log transformation, which compresses larger values and spreads smaller ones, was evaluated for its effectiveness in improving model fit, reducing residual skewness, and addressing heteroscedasticity, particularly for the ANCOVA model~\cite{hospitality2024log}. The results showed that for ANCOVA, the log transformation generally improved the satisfaction of linear regression assumptions and led to more robust coefficient estimates. For Random Forest, while less sensitive to distributional assumptions, the transformation sometimes contributed to marginal improvements in performance or stability.

A K-fold cross-validation strategy was employed to ensure the robustness and generalizability of the model performance estimates. This method involved partitioning the data into K subsets, training the model on K-1 subsets, and evaluating it on the held-out subset, repeating this process K times. This approach provides a more reliable estimate of how the models would perform on unseen data, mitigating the risk of overfitting to the training data.

Permutation Importance, by contrast, showed that shuffling these same features led to the most significant drop in model accuracy, confirming their substantial independent contribution to predictive power~\cite{ibm2025randomforest, numberanalytics2025importance}. This analysis highlighted that factors beyond simple population size, such as housing market dynamics and demographic composition, are crucial drivers of older adult homelessness.

\subsubsection{Synthesis Across Models}
A comprehensive synthesis of the insights from both models revealed common themes and highlighted nuanced relationships. Features like population size, median rent, and the proportion of the 65+ population consistently emerged as highly influential across both Random Forest's importance rankings and ANCOVA's significant coefficients. This strong agreement provides robust evidence that these factors are fundamental drivers of older adult homelessness in California, aligning with existing research that points to housing affordability and economic conditions as primary contributors~\cite{ppic2025homelessness, huduser2019market, culhane2024learning}.

Any discrepancies between the models were carefully examined. For example, while CoC centroid latitude might show some importance in the Random Forest model (suggesting complex spatial patterns or regional clusters not captured by a simple linear relationship), its coefficient in ANCOVA might not be statistically significant. This indicates that its influence might be mediated by other factors or involve non-linear spatial effects (e.g., urban density effects) that a linear model cannot fully capture. This multi-model perspective offers a richer, more nuanced understanding of the factors driving older adult homelessness, helping to avoid oversimplified conclusions about feature importance~\cite{field2013discovering}. By synthesizing the feature importance from Random Forest and the coefficients from ANCOVA, the analysis moves beyond mere correlation to infer more robust causal pathways. For instance, the consistent identification of median rent as a major driver across both models provides strong evidence that higher rental costs are a direct and substantial contributor to increased homelessness, corroborating existing research~\cite{ppic2025homelessness, huduser2019market, culhane2024learning}.

\subsection{Policy Implications and Recommendations}

The findings from this data-driven assessment of CoC effectiveness carry significant policy implications for state and local governments, as well as CoC leadership in California. The analysis yields concrete, actionable recommendations aimed at mitigating and ultimately reducing homelessness among older adults.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Address Systemic Housing and Economic Factors:} The consistent emergence of housing affordability (e.g., median rent) and economic stability (e.g., unemployment rates) as significant predictors across both statistical models underscores the critical need to address these systemic factors as fundamental strategies to combat older adult homelessness~\cite{ppic2025homelessness, huduser2019market, terner2023older}. Policy efforts should prioritize increasing the supply of affordable housing, implementing rent stabilization measures, and expanding rental assistance programs to prevent older adults from falling into homelessness due to economic precarity.

    \item \textbf{Tailored Interventions for the 65+ Age Group:} The unique vulnerabilities of older adults, including their increased health challenges, financial precarity, and the prevalence of late-onset homelessness, necessitate tailored interventions~\cite{terner2023older}. Recommendations include:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Expanded Eviction Prevention Programs:} Given that financial factors often precede homelessness for this group, robust and accessible eviction prevention services, including legal aid and emergency financial assistance, are crucial.
        \item \textbf{Accessible Supportive Housing:} Development of housing models that integrate age-appropriate supportive services, addressing health, mobility, and social isolation needs.
        \item \textbf{Streamlined Benefit Access:} Efforts to connect older adults to all eligible benefits (e.g., Social Security, SSI, Medicare/Medicaid) to stabilize their income and healthcare access.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Leverage Data-Driven Decision-Making and Predictive Analytics:} The study demonstrates the power of combining public data with rigorous statistical analysis to derive actionable insights. State and local governments, and CoCs, should continue to invest in and refine data-driven decision-making processes and predictive analytics for early intervention and targeted prevention efforts. Successful models, such as the California Policy Lab's Homelessness Prevention Unit, which uses predictive modeling to identify and proactively reach out to high-risk individuals, offer a blueprint for effective, preventative strategies~\cite{capolicylab2025hpu}.

    \item \textbf{Facilitate Best Practice Sharing and Knowledge Transfer:} The identified high-performing CoCs, whose actual homelessness counts were lower than predicted under standardized conditions, represent valuable sources of knowledge. Mechanisms should be established to facilitate the sharing of their successful strategies, program designs, and operational efficiencies across the state. This could include regular forums, detailed case studies, and funding for peer-to-peer learning initiatives.

    \item \textbf{Enhance Accountability for Homelessness Program Funds:} Aligning with previous audit findings~\cite{ppic2025homelessness}, there is a clear need for increased accountability measures for homelessness program funds. The state should implement robust, standardized evaluation frameworks that track outcomes at the CoC level, ensuring that investments translate into measurable reductions in homelessness. This study’s methodology offers a potential framework for such evaluations. This section serves as the crucial bridge between academic research and practical policy. The recommendations are specific, actionable, and directly derived from the empirical findings, demonstrating the practical application of the mission to apply advanced analytical methods for positive social change.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Study Limitations}

Despite the rigorous methodology employed, this study has several limitations that warrant acknowledgment.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Data Limitations:} The primary homelessness data, derived from the Homelessness Data Integration System (HDIS), relies on individuals accessing services within CoCs~\cite{caopendatahomelessness}. This inherently leads to a potential undercounting of unsheltered individuals who do not engage with formal service systems~\cite{culhane2024learning, caopendatahomelessness}. Consequently, the reported homelessness counts may not fully represent the true scale of the problem, and CoCs that are more effective at outreach and engagement might appear to have higher counts due to better data capture. Specific CoC data gaps, such as the absence of data for Tehama and Lake Counties, and limitations for Yuba/Sutter and Alpine/Inyo/Mono CoCs, also affect the comprehensiveness of the dataset~\cite{caopendatahomelessness}. The reliance on reported service data might influence the representativeness of the homeless population captured, potentially skewing the understanding of "effectiveness." Explicitly stating these limitations is paramount for maintaining scientific integrity and credibility, managing reader expectations regarding the generalizability and certainty of the findings.

    \item \textbf{Model Limitations:} While both Random Forest and ANCOVA offer distinct advantages, they also have limitations. ANCOVA relies on assumptions such as homogeneity of regression slopes and normality of residuals, which, despite efforts at log transformation, may not be perfectly met across all CoCs~\cite{realstatsancova, aatbioquestancova}. Random Forest, while powerful, can sometimes be perceived as a "black box" model, making the direct causal interpretation of individual feature effects challenging, even with feature importance metrics~\cite{molnar2022interpretable}. The study's efforts to enhance interpretability through the prediction set approach and comparative analysis mitigate, but do not entirely eliminate, this challenge.

    \item \textbf{Causation vs. Correlation:} Even with the "prediction set" approach, which aims to approximate a controlled comparison by standardizing confounding variables, the study primarily identifies correlations rather than definitive causal relationships. While the methodology allows for a more equitable comparison of CoC performance by isolating the impact of local strategies from exogenous factors, it does not fully control for all unobserved variables or complex feedback loops inherent in social systems~\cite{molnar2022interpretable}.

    \item \textbf{Geographical Data Simplification:} The representation of complex spatial patterns through simple CoC centroids (latitude/longitude) and a binary adjacency matrix is a simplification. While valuable, it may not fully capture the nuances of geographical influence, such as varying population densities within CoCs, or the strength of connectivity between neighboring areas (e.g., shared transportation networks, economic ties). More sophisticated spatial econometric models could further refine this aspect.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future Research Directions}

Building upon the findings and limitations of this study, several avenues for future research emerge:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Incorporation of More Granular Data:} Future research could explore the integration of even more granular data, such as block-group level ACS data, to refine population estimates for CoCs, particularly for those that subdivide counties or include unincorporated areas. This would enhance the precision of population denominators and improve the accuracy of per-capita homelessness rates.
    \item \textbf{Dynamic Analysis and Time-Series Modeling:} Extending the current cross-sectional analysis to a longitudinal study, incorporating time-series data on homelessness counts and relevant covariates, would enable the investigation of trends, the impact of policy changes over time, and the dynamic effectiveness of CoCs. This could involve panel data models or more advanced time-series forecasting techniques.
    \item \textbf{Qualitative and Mixed-Methods Research:} To complement the quantitative findings, future research could incorporate qualitative methods, such as in-depth interviews with CoC leaders, service providers, and individuals experiencing homelessness. This would provide richer contextual understanding of the factors driving effectiveness or challenges, explaining the "why" behind the statistical patterns.
    \item \textbf{Exploration of Additional Predictors:} Expanding the set of predictor variables to include more detailed information on CoC funding allocations, specific program types and capacities (e.g., number of rapid re-housing units, prevention slots), local housing policies (e.g., zoning laws, rent control), and healthcare access could provide a more comprehensive understanding of CoC performance.
    \item \textbf{Advanced Spatial Econometrics:} To more fully account for spatial dependencies and spillover effects, future work could employ advanced spatial econometric models that explicitly model spatial autocorrelation in the error terms or the dependent variable, providing a more nuanced understanding of how neighboring CoCs influence each other.
    \item \textbf{Causal Inference Techniques:} Exploring advanced causal inference techniques, such as difference-in-differences or instrumental variables, where appropriate data allows, could help move beyond correlation to establish more definitive causal links between CoC interventions and homelessness outcomes.
    \item \textbf{Bias Detection and Fairness in AI Models:} Further research into the interpretability of machine learning models for social policy, focusing on detecting and mitigating biases within the data and model predictions, would be crucial. This aligns with the principles of ethical AI development and ensures that policy recommendations do not inadvertently perpetuate existing inequities.
\end{enumerate}
